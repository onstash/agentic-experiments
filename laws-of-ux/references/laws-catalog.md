# Laws of UX catalog

Source: [Laws of UX llms.txt](https://lawsofux.com/llms.txt)

The working knowledge below is local and does not require network access. Each law links to the canonical page for attribution, origins, takeaways, and further reading.

- [Aesthetic-Usability Effect](https://lawsofux.com/aesthetic-usability-effect/): People often perceive attractive interfaces as easier to use. Use visual polish to support clarity, never to hide broken behavior.
- [Choice Overload](https://lawsofux.com/choice-overload/): Too many options overwhelm decision-making. Show the smallest useful set of choices and reveal advanced options progressively.
- [Chunking](https://lawsofux.com/chunking/): Group related pieces into meaningful units. Use sections, summaries, and bounded lists for dense information.
- [Cognitive Bias](https://lawsofux.com/cognitive-bias/): Systematic thinking errors affect perception and decisions. Check defaults, framing, confirmation, and status language for predictable bias.
- [Cognitive Load](https://lawsofux.com/cognitive-load/): Interfaces consume limited mental resources. Remove unnecessary steps, jargon, memory requirements, and unrelated information.
- [Doherty Threshold](https://lawsofux.com/doherty-threshold/): Productive interaction depends on fast feedback, commonly described as under 400 ms. Show immediate acknowledgement and meaningful progress for slower work.
- [Fitts's Law](https://lawsofux.com/fittss-law/): Target acquisition depends on target size and distance. Make frequent actions large, reachable, and separated from destructive actions.
- [Flow](https://lawsofux.com/flow/): Deep focus benefits from clear goals, low interruption, and immediate feedback. Protect the active-work surface from unnecessary navigation and prompts.
- [Goal-Gradient Effect](https://lawsofux.com/goal-gradient-effect/): Motivation tends to increase as a goal gets closer. Show meaningful progress and the next achievable step.
- [Hick's Law](https://lawsofux.com/hicks-law/): Decision time increases with the number and complexity of choices. Prefer one clear primary action and sensible defaults.
- [Jakob's Law](https://lawsofux.com/jakobs-law/): People expect familiar interaction patterns from other products. Use standard controls, placement, terminology, and behavior unless there is a strong reason not to.
- [Law of Common Region](https://lawsofux.com/law-of-common-region/): Elements inside a defined boundary are perceived as related. Group controls and their feedback in the same surface.
- [Law of Proximity](https://lawsofux.com/law-of-proximity/): Nearby elements are perceived as related. Put labels, inputs, errors, and actions close to the thing they affect.
- [Law of Prägnanz](https://lawsofux.com/law-of-pr%C3%A4gnanz/): People interpret ambiguous complexity as the simplest plausible form. Make hierarchy and state explicit rather than requiring inference.
- [Law of Similarity](https://lawsofux.com/law-of-similarity/): Similar visual elements are perceived as belonging together. Use consistent styling for equivalent actions and states.
- [Law of Uniform Connectedness](https://lawsofux.com/law-of-uniform-connectedness/): Visually connected elements are perceived as related. Use shared containers, lines, or state transitions to show relationships.
- [Mental Model](https://lawsofux.com/mental-model/): Users act from their understanding of how a system works. Use product language and behavior that match the user's task model.
- [Miller's Law](https://lawsofux.com/millers-law/): Working memory can hold only a limited number of items at once. Reduce simultaneous items and group information; do not treat 7±2 as a target count.
- [Occam's Razor](https://lawsofux.com/occams-razor/): When explanations predict equally well, prefer the one with fewer assumptions. Choose the smallest state model and simplest interaction that explains the evidence.
- [Paradox of the Active User](https://lawsofux.com/paradox-of-the-active-user/): People usually start using software instead of reading instructions. Make the first action obvious and provide guidance at the moment of need.
- [Pareto Principle](https://lawsofux.com/pareto-principle/): A minority of causes often produces most effects. Prioritize the small set of flows that dominate real use and failure impact.
- [Parkinson's Law](https://lawsofux.com/parkinsons-law/): Work expands to fill available time. Use bounded scopes, timeboxes, and explicit review points.
- [Peak-End Rule](https://lawsofux.com/peak-end-rule/): People judge an experience strongly by its peak and ending. Make completion, recovery, and handoff states clear and reassuring.
- [Postel's Law](https://lawsofux.com/postels-law/): Accept reasonable input variations while sending precise, predictable output. Normalize safe input and validate at system boundaries.
- [Selective Attention](https://lawsofux.com/selective-attention/): Attention focuses on a subset of available stimuli. Emphasize the user's current goal and suppress unrelated status or metadata.
- [Serial Position Effect](https://lawsofux.com/serial-position-effect/): People remember the beginning and end of a sequence best. Put setup guidance first and the next action or confirmation last.
- [Tesler's Law](https://lawsofux.com/teslers-law/): Some complexity is inherent and cannot be removed. Put unavoidable complexity in the system, not in the user's working memory.
- [Von Restorff Effect](https://lawsofux.com/von-restorff-effect/): A visually distinct item is more likely to be remembered. Reserve emphasis for the most important action, warning, or difference.
- [Working Memory](https://lawsofux.com/working-memory/): Temporary memory is limited while users complete a task. Preserve context, show current state, and avoid requiring users to remember previous screens.
- [Zeigarnik Effect](https://lawsofux.com/zeigarnik-effect/): People remember interrupted or unfinished tasks. Provide resume context, unfinished-work visibility, and a clear way to continue or close the loop.
